Abstract

State tax structures shape where companies expand, how they invest and what it costs them to operate. This thesis tracks how corporate tax structures in each state changed from 2018 to 2023, ranks states by their average corporate tax rate, and compares that ranking with CNBC's assessment of the best states to start a business. The goal is practical guidance for businesses, policymakers and investors deciding where to operate.

Research questions

  1. How have state corporate tax structures changed from 2018 to 2023?
  2. How have those changes affected business decisions, especially geographic expansion?
  3. What strategies can organizations use to optimize operations across different tax regimes?
  4. What correlation exists between tax structure and in-state economic growth?

Key findings

No corporate tax

Nevada, Ohio, Texas and Washington held a 0% corporate income tax rate in every year studied. Ohio and Washington tax gross receipts instead of income. South Dakota and Wyoming levy no corporate tax at all.

Highest rates

Minnesota (9.8%), Illinois (9.5%) and Pennsylvania (9.99% through 2022) stayed at the costly end. Pennsylvania's cut to 8.99% in 2023 left Minnesota with the highest rate.

Taxes aren't everything

CNBC's top state for business was Virginia in 2021 and North Carolina in 2022–23. Neither was among the lowest-tax states except North Carolina (2.5%). A regression on CNBC's ten categories explained 95.7% of the variation in state rank (adjusted R², n = 50).

New York City

Despite its reputation, New York ranked 27th of 51 on average corporate tax (6.5%) until 2021, slipping to 32nd at 6.88% in 2022. Its CNBC rank moved from 22nd (2021) to 36th (2022) and back to 20th (2023), and the forecast shows it holding steady.
